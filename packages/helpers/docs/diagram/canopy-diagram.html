Corporate Empire and Cartographic Authority

On the East India Company and its maps

By the early nineteenth century, the East India Company had transformed from a trading enterprise into a territorial power, exercising corporate sovereignty across much of the Indian subcontinent. Maps such as Pinkerton’s Hindostan helped naturalize that expansion1 by translating diverse political landscapes into legible borders, routes, and regions that could be governed, taxed, and defended. The atlas format reinforced these claims by presenting Company influence as stable geography rather than contested rule.

This cartographic vision also reveals how knowledge production and imperial power worked together. By privileging standardized measurement, European place-name conventions, and administrative divisions, the map frames the subcontinent as a coherent space open to managerial control. Such representations supported both practical governance and public legitimacy, offering metropolitan readers an image of order and possession that obscured local authority, resistance, and the uneven realities of Company rule.

The surveyors themselves knew the borders were unsettled. One wrote from Calcutta that the Rajpootana districts were “by no means settled”2 and begged his correspondent not to print them as though they were, a caution the published atlases rarely kept.

1. “Hindostan,” in John Pinkerton, A Modern Atlas (1811). Northwestern University Libraries.
2. Letter to Mr Edgecombe, Calcutta, 14 March 1812. NU MS 127, box 4.
Scholarly Essay
Calcutta, 14th March 1812
Dear Mr Edgecombe,
I have the honour to enclose the
surveys of the Rajpootana districts,
which the Company’s atlas leaves
unfinished. The borders there are
by no means settled, and I beg you
not to print them as though they were.
Your obedient servant,
J. Hartley
